Where the Red Fern Grows Message

Introduction

The book shows us a myth! Allegory? This message started looking pretty easy. Then a sister in Christ helped me tamp it down, and then I found out the trap was a myth.

Come on.

But through the Holy Spirit, I found that this book shows us a clever myth that exposes a very real truth about the human heart.

In this message, I hope to explore the spiritual parallel the book showcases: the folklore raccoon trap.

Story Time: A boy, his two dogs, and one big adventure. Billy has long dreamed of owning not one but two dogs, so when he's finally able to save up enough money for his two pups to call his own, he's ecstatic. Soon Billy and his hounds become the finest hunting team in the valley, but tragedy awaits these determined hunters, now friends, and in time Billy learns that hope can grow out of despair. Where the Red Fern Grows is a beloved classic that captures the powerful bond between man and man's best friend. It will stand the test of time as long as there are boys and girls who love their dogs and dogs who love them.

Now to the point of the book, chapter 7.

Chapter seven illustrates my point. It starts, "It seems that the worries and wants of a young boy never cease. Now that I had my pups, another obstacle had cropped up. This one looked absolutely impossible. I have to have a coon skin so I can train them."

With a mischievous little smile on his face, he said, "You wouldn't think a fellow could catch a coon with this brace and bit, would you?" I thought he was kidding me, and it made me feel bad. "Why, Grandpa," I said, "you couldn't catch a coon in a jillion years with that thing. You just don't have any idea how smart they are." "Yes, you can," he said. "You bet your boots you can. Why, when I was a boy, I caught coons on top of coons with one of these things."

I saw Grandpa was serious and I got interested. He laid down the brace on the counter, picked up a small paper sack, and filled it about half full of horse nails. "Now you do everything exactly as I tell you," he said, "and you'll catch that coon." "Yes, sir, Grandpa," I said. "I will. I'll do anything to catch one of them."

"Now the first thing you'll need is some bright objects," he said. "The best thing is a bright, shiny bottle cap, a little smaller than this bit. Do you understand?" I nodded my head. "Now," he said, "you go down along the river where there are a lot of coon tracks. Find a good, solid log close by and bore a hole down about 6 in. Drop one of these bright pieces of bottle cap in the hole and be sure it's laying right on the bottom." I was all ears. I didn't want to miss one word my grandfather said.

"Now, we'll say this is the hole you bored in the log," he said. "About an inch apart, drive these nails in on a slant opposite each other." Holding one of the nails in his right hand, he showed me the right angle. "The ends of the nails will enter the hole about halfway between the top and the piece of bottle cap." He continued, "leave an opening between the sharp points big enough for a coon to get his paw through." He asked me if I understood.

I asked, "How is that going to catch a coon, Grandpa?" "It'll catch him all right," he said, "and it won't fail. You see, a coon is a curious little animal. Anything that is bright and shiny attracts him. He will reach in and pick it up. When his paws close on the bright bottle cap, it balls up, and when he starts to pull it from the hole, the sharp ends of the nail will gouge into his paws and he's caught."

There are three ways I'm going to attempt skinning this cat:

  • Share what I've learned in my life and raising youth that aligns with this mythical raccoon trap. And Billy as our example.
  • Show some of my "foils" and shiny "bottle caps" to help you identify any in your life.
  • And the third, leave you with my final thought from Alan and how this trap ties in to our lives.

Part 1: My Youthful Stubbornness

As I look back, I would say my first sighting of a "bottle cap" was way back in 1987. I seen what fear and intimidation, the biker lifestyle, did to other "dads" and "school kids" at a parade in downtown Springfield.

Just being seen with my stepdad changed the behavior in a school bully. It was then I grabbed my first "bottle cap". That directed my path from my youth to adulthood.

Now raising my two boys shown me how effective this simple trap can be. The toy they obsess about, the game that all the other kids have (which when they get it find out its lame and never play it again). Even Billy, in the book, thought that getting two pups would solve all of his problems and make his life perfect.

We see here on page 24, when Billy approaches his grandfather and gives the money he'd been saving in secret for over two years, his grandpa was dumbfounded. His jaw hit the floor. At first, his grandpa thought he had stolen the money, but his grandpa's heart sank after he found out he had been saving the money for two long years. As we see here, Billy had neglected his haircuts, he had worn his shoes completely out and not bought new ones, and put all of his money into getting these two dogs.

As I reread this book and made this message, I couldn't help but look back on my life and see so many foolish bottle caps that I had grabbed a hold of, particularly material bottle caps that I felt would solve some fundamental hole in my life, but to outside observers, they could see a simple ringtail in a mythical trap.

And to segue from part one into part two, as I showed you, my first bottle cap concept was fear and intimidation. That directed my path from my youth to adulthood. And what I'd like to say is don't let this follow you into adulthood. That's where paradigms are formed.

For those of you that don't know what a paradigm is, here is the definition:

A set of assumptions, concepts, values, and practices that constitutes a way of viewing reality for the community that shares them, especially in an intellectual discipline.

Part Three: Final Thoughts form a song I listen to about "Thinking to much" being a Bottlecap

A person who thinks all the time has nothing to think about except thoughts. So, he loses touch with reality and lives in a world of illusions.

By thoughts I mean specifically, chatter in the skull—perpetual and compulsive repetition of words, of reckoning and calculating.

I'm not saying that thinking is bad. Like everything else, it's useful in moderation. A good servant, but a bad master.

And all so-called civilized peoples have increasingly become crazy and self-destructive because through excessive thinking they have lost touch with reality. That's to say, we confuse signs with the real world.

This is the beginning of meditation.

Most of us would have rather money than tangible wealth. And a great occasion is somehow spoiled for us unless photographed, and to read about it the next day in the newspaper is oddly more fun for us than the original event.

This is a disaster. For as a result of confusing the real world of nature with mere signs, we are destroying nature. We are so tied up in our minds that we've lost our senses.

Time to wake up.

What is reality? Obviously, no one can say, because it isn't words. It isn't material, that's just an idea. Reality is... The point cannot be explained in words.

The Illusion of the Bait

In the text, Alan speaks about living in a "world of illusions" and confusing "signs with the real world." In Where the Red Fern Grows, Grandpa's trap uses a shiny object—a bright bottlecap—to catch the raccoon. The shiny object isn't real food; it is merely an illusion of value, much like the temporary high of meth or the false promises of addiction.

The Refusal to Let Go

A Person's/raccoon's downfall is its instinctual refusal to open its clenched fist and let go of the shiny bait once it reaches inside the log trap. Watts warns that "a good servant can become a bad master." When obsession or addiction takes over, the mind or body locks onto the shiny object, choosing to hold tight even when holding on leads directly to destruction.

"Lost Touch with Reality"

The text mentions becoming "self-destructive because through excessive thinking [or obsession] they have lost touch with reality." You can draw a direct line between the raccoon trapped by its own grip, a person trapped in the grip of addiction, and the spiritual call to let go of the "shiny objects" of the world before they trap us completely.

"Let us cast off every weight (bottlecaps) and sin (foil) that so easily ensnares you and run the race set before you, looking unto the author and finisher of our faith."
